# 8. The Targeting Reckoning: Can You Really Aim Your Growth?

## The Keystone Error: From EMG and Pump to Predicted Growth

The last chapter left us with a quiet, unsettling lesson: a set that *feels* harder is not a set that *works* better. Drop sets, supersets, and rest-pause burn more, spike more lactate, and read higher on every effort scale a lifter can sense in the moment — and they grow exactly as much muscle as plain straight sets once the volume is matched. The sensation was real. The extra growth was imaginary. The mistake was trusting an acute signal to forecast a chronic outcome.

This chapter is that same mistake wearing a different costume. Instead of asking *does this set-structure build more muscle?*, the targeting question asks *does this grip, this angle, this stance, this signature exercise build more muscle in this specific place?* It is the most beloved game in the gym. Wide grip for the lats, close grip for the "inner back." Incline for the upper chest, decline for the lower. Sumo stance for glutes, narrow stance for quads. Hip thrust because the glutes "fire" so much harder than in a squat. Every one of these beliefs has the same shape, and every one rests on the same single, load-bearing assumption — that you can look at how loudly a muscle responds in the moment and predict where it will grow over months.

That assumption has a name in the corpus, and a verdict: **refuted**. The claim that acute muscle activation — or the acute pump, the swelling you feel mid-set — reliably predicts which exercise causes more regional growth does not survive scrutiny. It is the keystone of this chapter, because nearly every targeting claim is an arch built on top of it. Knock out the keystone and most of the arch comes down with it.

Two terms do the heavy lifting here, so let me define them plainly. **EMG** — electromyography — is a measurement of the electrical chatter a muscle gives off when the nervous system calls on it, usually reported as a percentage of the most you can produce in an all-out effort (your maximum voluntary contraction). It is, in effect, a microphone held to a muscle: it tells you how loudly that muscle is being *asked* to work right now. The **pump**, more formally acute muscle swelling, is the temporary thickening you can see on an ultrasound and feel in the mirror after a hard set, driven by blood and fluid pooling in the worked tissue. Both are seductive precisely because they are immediate and vivid. You can feel them. And both have been quietly demoted to *mechanism* — interesting clues about what a muscle is doing in the moment, not valid predictors of what it will become.

The reason is simply that, when researchers have measured all three things at once — activation, swelling, and actual growth, the last one tracked by **MRI** (magnetic resonance imaging, which measures real changes in muscle volume over a training program) — the three point in different directions. The correlations between an exercise's acute activation and the growth it eventually produces are weak and inconsistent, ranging from roughly zero to modest, occasionally even slightly negative. A muscle on too little volume to grow at all can still deliver a spectacular pump. An exercise can light up the EMG microphone and grow no more than a quieter one. The activation premise of a targeting claim is often perfectly true; it is the leap from *activates more* to *grows more* that fails. Hold onto that distinction — *activation premise true, growth conclusion unsupported* — because it is the precise fault line running through almost every claim below.

## The Glutes: Hip Thrust vs. Squat

Start with the most confident targeting claim in modern lifting: the barbell hip thrust is the king of glute exercises because it produces so much more gluteal activation than the squat. The activation half of that sentence is flatly true and well measured. In trained women lifting near a ten-rep max, the hip thrust elicited roughly 70% of maximum upper-glute EMG against the squat's 30% — more than double — and review data put the gap at something like 75% versus 53%. If EMG forecast growth, the case would be closed.

It doesn't, and so the case isn't. The claim that the hip thrust is therefore *superior for growing the glutes* is **refuted**, on exactly the keystone error. When the growth study finally arrived — training matched for volume and effort, glutes measured directly — squats and hip thrusts produced broadly similar gluteus maximus growth. The two-to-one activation advantage simply did not translate into a two-to-one, or any, growth advantage. Two details sharpen the point. First, the hip thrust is not even the highest-activation glute exercise on offer; step-ups beat both, and nobody crowns the step-up. Second, the squat loads the gluteus maximus at a *longer muscle length* — deep hip flexion stretches the muscle under load — and length, as this chapter will show, is the one lever that actually moves regional growth. If anything, that mechanism nudges the squat ahead, not behind.

None of this means the hip thrust is a bad exercise. This is the place to separate the two questions the rest of the book keeps separating. On the **effect** question — does it grow more glute than the squat? — the answer is no; they are roughly equivalent, with the squat arguably ahead on length. On the **practical** question — is it worth doing anyway? — the answer is an easy yes. The hip thrust loads the glutes hard at their *shortened*, peak-contracted position, costs the spine and the whole system very little fatigue, and is trivially easy to load and progress. That short-length emphasis is genuinely something the squat does not provide, which gives rise to a gentler, more defensible claim: that hip thrust and squat are *complementary* — one loading the glute short, the other loading it long — so a program might reasonably include both for fuller coverage. The corpus rates that complementarity idea **disputed**, not refuted, and the distinction is honest. The anatomy is real and the programming logic is sound, but no head-to-head trial has shown that combining the two grows *more* total glute than doing more of either alone. It is a reasonable heuristic for coverage, not a proven recipe for extra size. The hip thrust earns its place for what it adds — tension where the squat offers little — not for a growth crown it never actually won.

## The Back: Grips, Widths, and Lat-Targeting Folklore

If the glutes show the keystone error in a single exercise, the back shows an entire industry built on it. No body part attracts more targeting folklore. Wide grip to "spread the wings," close grip for thickness, underhand to "hit the lower lats," neutral for the "inner back," rows for the mid-back and pulldowns for the lats — each grip and angle sold as a dial that re-aims growth around the latissimus dorsi. The corpus tested this cluster claim by claim, and it collapses almost in unison.

The cleanest evidence is a study of forty trained men performing seven different pulldown variants — varying grip type, grip width, and forearm orientation — at a meaningful load. Across all seven, latissimus dorsi activation simply *did not differ significantly*; the lat held at roughly the same level regardless of how the bar was held. The authors said the quiet part out loud: their data challenge the very assumption that grip targets this muscle. On the strength of that and the supporting literature, the claim that grip type, width, and orientation meaningfully bias which region of the lat is trained is **refuted**. What grip actually changes is *synergist* loading — the biceps, the rear deltoid, the traps — along with comfort, joint stress, and leverage. Those are real, and they are reasons to vary grips. They are not the lat being re-aimed.

The sub-claims fall the same way. A wide pronated grip preferentially recruiting or growing the lat over a closer grip: **refuted** — the activation edge is small, inconsistent, and in one of its source studies the authors themselves judged it likely "of no weight-training significance," with no growth trial behind it. The front pulldown being superior to the behind-the-neck version for building lats: **refuted** — the one study favoring the front used about 18% more load in that condition (which inflates its EMG) and the lat advantage appears in only one phase of the rep, reversing in the other. The underhand-equals-arms, overhand-equals-back rule: **refuted** on its own terms, because when measured, biceps *and* lat activation came out statistically identical across pronated and supinated grips. The rule fails even at the activation level it was invented to describe.

Two broader back beliefs deserve their own mention because they feel less like folklore and more like settled coaching wisdom. The first: rows bias the mid-back (the rhomboids and mid-trapezius) while pulldowns bias the lats. The second: rows are a *poor* lat exercise because the lat loses leverage at the top, so real lat work should chase long-length movements like straight-arm pullovers. Both are **refuted** as growth claims — and here the nuance matters, because both contain a kernel of truth that explains their staying power. Rows do recruit the mid-back musculature more on EMG, and the leverage argument against rows for lats is biomechanically coherent. But the row-versus-pulldown split rests on activation gathered at light loads, where the highest-threshold fibers aren't even in play, and there is no regional-growth trial behind it; the anti-row argument is explicitly acknowledged as pure mechanism, with no equated comparison showing pullovers grow lats more than rows do. As *directional emphasis* and *exercise variety*, including both horizontal and vertical pulls is perfectly sensible. As a claim that you are steering where the back grows, it is unproven. Rows still load the lats substantially; dismissing them is mechanism dressed as measurement.

## The Chest: Incline, Flat, Decline, and the Upper-Pec Question

The chest is where the targeting story finally gets interesting, because here one claim — and only one — has a thread of real growth data behind it, while its neighbors do not.

Take the easy refutations first. The belief that flat bench pressing biases growth of the sternal, mid-portion of the pectoralis major is **refuted**: the single volume-matched growth trial found no mid-pec advantage for flat pressing over incline at the relevant measurement sites. The belief that dips and decline pressing preferentially grow the lower, costal pec is **refuted** on the same grounds — the lower-pec bias exists only on the EMG microphone, with no longitudinal trial showing more lower-pec growth than flat bench, and the one regional-pec growth study never even tested decline or dips. And the popular program-builder's rule that combining incline *and* flat pressing maximizes overall pec growth versus doing either alone is **refuted** by the very trial people invoke for it: in that study the combination group grew the upper pec *less* than incline-only and merely matched flat for the mid-pec. Combining angles is a reasonable way to hedge your regional coverage; it is not a demonstrated path to *more* chest.

That leaves the famous one: incline pressing biases and grows the upper, clavicular head of the pec more than flat pressing. This claim is **disputed** — and the word is chosen carefully, because incline is the rare regional-bench claim that does not rest on EMG alone. There is a volume-matched longitudinal finding in its corner: a 2020 trial reported that incline-only training grew the upper pec more than flat *and* more than the combination. That is genuine growth data pointing in the claimed direction, which is why it is not refuted outright. But lean on that single study and it wobbles. It was small, in untrained subjects, training once weekly for eight weeks, with only about ten people per group for the thickness measurement and a frankly implausible outlier value the reviewers flagged. The activation comparison at the two relevant angles was not even statistically significant, and the acute swelling data pointed the other way. So the honest verdict is a divided one: incline is a defensible, widely sensible tool for *emphasizing* the upper chest — the direction is plausible and the mechanism is partitioned anatomy doing real work — but the confident assertion that it *grows* the upper pec more hangs on one thin study and falls short of the trained, replicated evidence that would let us call it supported. Use incline because the upper chest is a legitimate region to attend to and the direction is reasonable. Don't sell it as a settled fact.

## Shoulders: Behind-the-Neck and Cable-Raise Claims

The shoulder gives us two claims that fail in two different, instructive ways.

The first is the behind-the-neck press, long pitched as a superior delt builder over the front or military press. Its verdict is **refuted**, and notably it is refuted from *empty* — there is no hypertrophy or strength trial in any population showing behind-the-neck pressing beats front pressing, which loads the same front and side deltoid through a safer path. Worse, this is one of the few cases where the practical question doesn't merely fail to rescue the effect claim but actively argues against it: forcing the shoulder into maximal external rotation and horizontal abduction loads the joint in a more vulnerable position, raising impingement and anterior-capsule stress, especially for anyone with limited shoulder mobility. A claim with no growth evidence *and* a worse risk profile has nothing to recommend it. Front and behind-the-neck pressing are at best equivalent for the delts; the superiority claim is unsupported.

The second is subtler and more modern: cable lateral raises are superior to dumbbell lateral raises because the cable keeps tension on the side delt at the bottom, stretched position, where a dumbbell offers almost none. This one is **refuted** as a *growth-superiority* claim — and yet it is the most defensible refuted claim in the chapter, which is worth sitting with. The mechanism is genuinely sound: loading a muscle in its lengthened position is, as the next section shows, the one targeting lever with real outcome data behind it, and a dumbbell does indeed go slack at the bottom of a lateral raise while a cable does not. The problem is purely evidentiary. There is no cable-versus-dumbbell lateral-raise growth study; the advantage is extrapolated from other muscles and largely untrained data. So the corpus refuses to certify "superior for growing the side delt" while fully endorsing the cable as a *reasonable choice* for its constant-tension resistance profile. The two are most likely comparable for matched-volume growth, with the cable offering a tension-profile edge that simply hasn't been shown to cash out in extra size. This is the effect-versus-practical split at its most precise: a great practical reason to pick the cable, riding a mechanism we believe in, that has nonetheless never been measured to grow more muscle.

## The Squat-Style and Foot-Position Family

Lower-body targeting claims form their own family, and they share a family resemblance: every one is **disputed**, none is supported, and they all break in the same place. Deep squats build more leg than shallow. A wide stance shifts work to the glutes and adductors, a narrow stance to the quads. A knee-dominant "sit straight down" squat builds quads better than a hip-dominant "sit back" style. Foot position on the leg press — high and wide for glutes and hamstrings, low and narrow for quads — lets you re-aim the machine. A deficit or step Romanian deadlift, by adding stretch, grows the posterior chain more than a standard one.

Read those again and notice what they have in common. Each is a claim about *redistributing* load within a movement, and each rests on EMG or biomechanical reasoning with no head-to-head growth trial to settle it. There is a single principle that explains why this whole family lands in "disputed" rather than "supported," and it is worth stating cleanly: at a given depth or joint configuration, the *summed* demand on the knee extensors and hip extensors is essentially fixed. Stance width, foot placement, and squat style change how that fixed total is *distributed* across the muscles — they do not add net stimulus out of nowhere. So when someone widens their stance and feels their glutes more, they are likely feeling a real shift in distribution; what they cannot claim is that they've conjured extra growth that a narrower stance would have missed. The acute evidence even rebels in places: when load is properly equated, partial squats have produced *higher* glute activation than deep ones, the reverse of the folklore.

This is not nihilism about lower-body exercise selection. Two of these claims point in directions the evidence broadly endorses, which is exactly why they're disputed and not refuted. Deeper squats almost certainly do grow the quads more — but partly because a deeper range does more mechanical work per rep (a volume effect riding inside the depth claim) and partly through longer muscle length, with the trained-population evidence still thin. A knee-dominant style genuinely keeps the quads loaded through more knee-flexion range, which is a legitimate, length-based reason to favor it for the quads. The deficit and step RDL borrow real support from the broader long-length literature. In each case the *direction* is reasonable and the underlying length mechanism is one we'll endorse in a moment. What's missing is the specific, measured, head-to-head growth result that would let any of them graduate from "well-reasoned heuristic" to "established fact." Choose your squat depth and stance for comfort, leverage, and which muscle you want to emphasize through its range — good reasons, all of them — just not under the illusion that foot angle is a growth dial with data behind it.

## What Actually Survived: Length-Biased Selection

After all that demolition, a fair reader might conclude that exercise selection doesn't matter at all — that any movement is as good as any other and the whole targeting enterprise is a fraud. That would be overcorrecting. A handful of targeting claims did survive the corpus's adversarial review and earned the rare verdict of **supported**. What makes them remarkable is that they all won the same way, through the same door — and it is not the door the gym usually knocks on. They won on *muscle length*, measured by *MRI*, in trials that matched volume and effort. Not activation. Not the pump. Length.

The cleanest example is the calf. Standing calf raises grow the gastrocnemius — the larger, two-jointed calf muscle that crosses both knee and ankle — far more than seated calf raises, while the soleus beneath it grows about the same either way. This is **supported**, and the numbers are striking because they come from a direct twelve-week trial with identical reps, sets, and load on both legs. Standing (knee straight, gastrocnemius stretched) grew the lateral gastrocnemius about 12% against the seated condition's under 2%, the medial head about 9% against well under 1% — large, unambiguous effects — while the soleus crept up a couple of percent regardless of knee angle. The reason is anatomy: with the knee bent in the seated position, the two-jointed gastrocnemius goes slack and can't be loaded at length, so it barely grows; the single-joint soleus doesn't care about the knee and grows either way. The practical translation is exact and useful: train the gastrocnemius standing, with the knee straight, and use the seated raise mainly to reach the soleus. "Seated also builds the gastrocnemius" is, on this evidence, essentially false.

The hamstrings give two survivors. First, the seated leg curl grows the hamstrings more than the lying leg curl — **supported** — because three of the four hamstring muscles also extend the hip, so the flexed-hip seated position holds them at a longer length under load. The MRI trial that found this also caught a beautiful internal check: the sartorius, which flexes the hip and is therefore *more* stretched in the lying position, grew more there. Every muscle grew most where it was trained longest. Second, and broader, hip-hinge movements like the Romanian deadlift emphasize the hip-extensor hamstring heads while leg curls emphasize the knee-flexor heads, so both patterns are needed for complete hamstring development — also **supported**, because here the activation map and the growth data agree, and the split tracks each muscle head's actual job at the hip versus the knee. The lesson for a program is concrete: a hinge and a curl are not redundant, and you want both.

The quads contribute the reclined leg extension. Performing leg extensions with the hips extended back (reclined, rather than upright at ninety degrees) substantially increases growth of the rectus femoris — the one quad muscle that also crosses the hip — without costing growth in the single-joint vastus lateralis. **Supported**, again from a direct MRI trial, again because the reclined position lengthens the two-joint rectus femoris while leaving the single-joint quad unaffected. And the arms contribute the curl: lengthened-biased curls that load the stretched position, such as incline dumbbell curls, build the biceps modestly more than shortened-position work like preacher curls. **Supported**, though with the honest caveat that the edge is small, partly extrapolated, and drawn largely from untrained lifters.

Stand these survivors next to each other and the pattern is unmistakable. They are unified by a single **supported** heuristic the corpus states directly: across the lower body, training a muscle toward its lengthened, stretched position tends to grow it somewhat more than training it short, so you should pick exercises that load the target muscle at long lengths. Three clarifications keep this honest, and each one is a guardrail against the very errors that sank the failed claims. It is almost certainly *not* the much-hyped "stretch-mediated hypertrophy" — the underlying mechanism is genuinely unresolved, and the dramatic chicken-wing stretch studies don't translate to a few extra seconds of stretch per set. You do *not* need to chase an extreme end-range "deep stretch" feeling to benefit; the lever is range and leverage, not sensation. And the magnitude is *modest* — on the order of a handful of percent in the relevant trials, mostly in untrained people, with a real possibility it shrinks in trained lifters. This is a genuine, qualified growth advantage, not a revolution.

Notice, finally, what these survivors have that the casualties lacked. Every supported claim is an *outcome* — muscle measured, by MRI, before and after, with volume and effort matched. Every refuted targeting claim leaned on a *proxy*: how loud the muscle got, how big the pump felt, how the leverage looked on paper. The hip thrust outshouts the squat and doesn't out-grow it. Grip variations outshout each other and grow the lat identically. But a standing calf raise, a seated leg curl, a reclined leg extension — these change the actual length the muscle works at, and length is the thing that turned out to matter. The reckoning isn't that targeting is impossible. It's that targeting works through length and leverage, not through activation and pump, and almost everyone has been aiming with the wrong instrument.

## The Full Scoreboard: 19 Refuted, 15 Disputed, 7 Supported, 5 Equivalent

Step back from the individual claims and look at the whole tally. The corpus put 46 popular training claims on trial under multiple skeptical lenses, and the verdicts distribute like this:

| Verdict | Count | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refuted | 19 | The claim, as stated, is contradicted by the evidence |
| Disputed | 15 | A kernel of truth, but overstated or resting on mechanism rather than outcome |
| Supported | 7 | Survived the lenses on measured outcomes |
| Equivalent | 5 | Real practical value, but no growth advantage at matched volume |

Seven supported out of forty-six. That is the headline, and it should reframe how you read confident fitness advice forever. The base rate of a strong, popular training claim turning out to be *true as stated* is low — under one in six. And the targeting claims fared worse than that average, clustering heavily in the refuted and disputed columns, while six of the seven supported claims are the length-biased selections from the previous section. The thing the gym argues about most is the thing the evidence supports least.

Why do so many confident claims fail in such similar ways? Four failure modes recur, and once you can name them you can run the test yourself on any claim you meet. The first and most common is the **activation-to-growth leap** — taking how hard a muscle fires or how big it pumps in the moment as proof of where it will grow over months. That single leap sinks the hip thrust crown, the entire grip-targeting cluster, the row-versus-pulldown split, the decline-for-lower-pec rule. The second is the **unmatched-volume confound**: a technique or variation looks superior only because, hidden inside the comparison, it sneaks in more work — more reps, more range, more mechanical effort per rep — so you're paying for ordinary volume and calling it magic. The deep-squat edge is partly this. The third is **mechanism standing in for measurement**: a biomechanical story so coherent — moment arms, lines of pull, leverage curves — that it feels like data, when no one has actually measured the growth. The anti-row argument, the leg-press foot-position claim, the cable lateral raise all live here. The fourth is **reliance on untrained, low-number samples**: even several of the *supported* claims carry this caveat honestly, because a single small trial in beginners is a starting point, not a settled law — which is exactly why "supported" here still means *supported with a confidence below certainty*, not *proven beyond doubt*.

Run those four tests and the whole landscape of this chapter organizes itself. Does the claim rest on activation or pump? Is the volume actually matched? Is there a measured outcome or only a mechanism? And was it measured in people like you? The claims that pass all four — standing calf raises for the gastrocnemius, the seated leg curl, the reclined leg extension, the hinge-and-curl hamstring split, lengthened curls, and the long-length heuristic underneath them all — you can aim with. The claims that fail are not always *wrong* to do; many are excellent exercises chosen for excellent practical reasons of comfort, coverage, and joints. They are just wrong about *why*. You are not re-aiming your growth with your grip. You are, at most, choosing where a muscle is loaded through its length — and choosing the rest for reasons that have nothing to do with growth at all, which is a perfectly good basis for a choice, as long as you know that's the basis.

Which raises the question this book has been circling. If selection mostly comes down to length, coverage, and a short list of practical considerations, then the largest practical consideration of all is the body doing the lifting — its history, its joints, its injuries, the movements it can't do today and the pain it shouldn't push through. A program that aims perfectly at a muscle a lifter can't load without flaring a bad shoulder has aimed at the wrong target entirely. The next chapter takes that seriously, and treats the individual body not as a footnote to the evidence but as the first input to it.
