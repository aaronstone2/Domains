# Thesis style guide — read before writing any chapter

This document governs voice, so the whole thesis reads as one mind, not ten. Follow it exactly.

## Who we're writing for
A smart, motivated lifter — curious and willing to follow a real argument, but **not** a scientist. Assume
intelligence, not prior jargon. Every technical term gets a plain-English definition the first time it appears.
The reader should finish each chapter feeling *"I finally understand why,"* not *"I've been told what."*

## The voice
- **Clear, confident, warm.** The register of the best science writing: an expert who has thought hard and
  wants you to *get* it. Authoritative without being stiff; vivid without being cute.
- **Second person where it earns its keep** ("if you add a fourth set…"), exposition otherwise. Contractions
  are fine. Short sentences for emphasis; longer ones to carry an idea. Vary the rhythm.
- **Prose first.** Turn data into sentences. A claim is not a table row — it's *"When researchers matched total
  volume, higher training frequency added essentially nothing, which quietly demolishes the 'hit it twice a week
  or waste it' rule."* Use a table or list only when it genuinely beats prose (a comparison, a worked set of loads).

## The intellectual moves that define this paper
1. **Honesty is the selling point.** When the evidence says *equivalent* or *refuted*, say so plainly — and then
   explain why the popular belief persists anyway. The reader's trust is earned by what we're willing to give up.
2. **Always separate the two questions.** The EFFECT question — *does it grow more muscle / build more strength?*
   — from the PRACTICAL question — *is it worth doing anyway, for time, fatigue, joints, or enjoyment?* Most fitness
   arguments are won by quietly swapping one for the other. We never do; we name both.
3. **Build from intuition to rigor.** Open a hard idea with a familiar picture or a question the reader already has,
   then earn the precise version. Analogies are encouraged when they illuminate (and flagged when they limp).
4. **Ground everything.** Abstractions ride on concrete examples drawn from the corpus: the gastrocnemius-vs-soleus
   calf split, the hip-thrust-vs-squat glute fight, the volume dose-response curve and its diminishing tail,
   the deadlift you don't take to failure.

## Evidence, in prose (not academic clutter)
- Reference the **grade and nature** of evidence inside the sentence: *"a 2026 meta-regression pooling 67 studies,"*
  *"a verdict that survived three independent skeptical re-reviews,"* *"only a mechanistic argument, never a growth
  measurement."* Convey the strength; skip inline citation cruft. A references section at the end carries the sources.
- The corpus tags each claim **supported / equivalent / disputed / refuted** with a recorded *nuance*. Honor those
  verdicts precisely — don't upgrade an "equivalent" into a "better."

## What NOT to do (AI tells — avoid)
- No "In conclusion," "It's important to note," "Furthermore," "Moreover," "It's worth mentioning," "Delve."
- No hedge-stacking ("it may potentially possibly suggest"). State the claim and its actual confidence.
- No empty throat-clearing or summarizing what you're about to say before you say it. Just say it.
- No bullet-list data dumps standing in for thought. No breathless hype. No fake balance ("some say X, others say Y")
  where the evidence actually leans — report the lean.

## Mechanics
- Markdown. Chapter starts with `# <number>. <Title>`, then flowing prose under `##` / `###` sections.
- Open each chapter with a hook that earns the reader's attention and states what the chapter will establish.
- End each chapter with a short bridge to the next idea (not a bullet "summary").
- Be generous with length — let ideas breathe — but every paragraph must carry weight. Comprehensive, never padded.
